SirenFirst: 10 custom architectures for ambulance-first traffic signals

A siren can be heard 100–300 m away, long before a camera can recognise the ambulance. SirenFirst uses that early audio warning, confirms it with vision and geometry, and gives the ambulance's approach arm a safe green signal. This page sets out ten designs built specifically for this problem and our small dataset.

Ground rules

Every architecture on this page follows these four rules.

No off-the-shelf detectorsNo YOLO, DETR, DINO, Faster R-CNN or Ultralytics. Each model is designed from basic blocks: convolutions, GRU/TCN, small attention layers and Kalman filters.
Trained from scratchNo downloaded weights, as the project brief requires.
Built for small data~1,300 labelled images, ~200 unique siren clips. Each design needs few labels, learns from unlabeled data, or gets labels from the other modality.
Safety firstAsymmetric fusion: audio may raise an alert, but only visual evidence plus geometry can change the signal.

The full system

The four layers stay the same. Only the Hear and See models change between the ten architectures, so all ten can be compared fairly on one benchmark.

The ten architectures

Each one is a complete audio + visual design with its own core idea.

01

MCX-Net — Multi-Cue eXplainable Network

Separate small heads look for the beacon, livery, "108"/red cross and body shape; an evidence gate combines them and shows which cue fired.

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Each head solves an easy sub-problem, so it needs few examples.
  • Beacon and livery cues are almost rule-based.
  • Explains every decision, matching the "explainable multi-cue" objective.
  • The required ablations (remove one cue at a time) come for free.

Risk

  • Cues fail when the lights are off or the livery is unusual. These become documented failure cases.

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Vision only has to confirm what audio already suspects, which is an easier problem.

Risk

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • A flashing beacon has a strong frequency signature that ordinary vehicles lack, so few labels are needed.
  • One design for both senses means less code and a clear story for the report.

Risk

  • Low frame-rate or heavily compressed CCTV can blur the flicker. We test at 10–25 FPS and report the results.
05

TubeTemporal-Net — Track-Tube (2+1)D Network

Stacks 16 frames of each tracked vehicle into a "tube" and learns shape, motion and flashing together.

TemporalMore samples per vehicle
flowchart LR
  T["Tracked vehicle"] --> TB["16-frame tube"] --> SP["2D spatial conv"] --> TP["1D temporal conv"] --> VE["Visual embedding"]
  A["3 s audio"] --> AT["1D temporal CNN"] --> AE["Audio embedding"]
  VE & AE --> GRU["GRU fusion
over last seconds"] --> O["Ambulance approaching?"]
  classDef k fill:#ffffff,stroke:#1b1f24,color:#1b1f24
  class T,TB,SP,TP,VE,A,AT,AE,GRU,O k

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • One passing vehicle gives dozens of overlapping tubes, so there are more training samples per label.
  • Flashing and steady approach are hard to fake from a single frame.

Risk

  • Heavier than single-frame models. Kept to ~1–2M parameters to hold ≥10 FPS.

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Built specifically for learning from a handful of examples per class.

  • Lower top accuracy than a normal classifier when data is plentiful, which is not our situation.

PartGraph-EV — Part-Whole Graph Reasoning

First finds the parts (lamp bar, red cross, 108 text, windscreen, wheels), then a small graph network checks whether they are arranged like an ambulance.

ExplainablePart-to-whole

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Parts are small patterns that appear many times, so they are easier to learn than whole vehicles.
  • The dataset already has part labels: ambulance_lamp, ambulance_108, ambulance_text.
  • Keeps the part-to-whole idea from the earlier capsule proposal without a pretrained YOLO.

Risk

  • Part labels are limited, and more coding is needed.

BEV-ArmNet — Bird's-Eye Arm-Aware Network

Warps the camera view to a top-down map first, then predicts the approach arm and ETA directly in metres.

Lane-lessDirect ETA

  F["Camera frame"] --> H["4-point homography"] --> B["Bird's-eye view"] --> CNN["Small BEV CNN"]
  CNN --> A1["Approach arm
N / E / S / W"]

  classDef k fill:#ffffff,stroke:#1b1f24,color:#1b1f24
  class F,H,B,CNN,A1,A2,A3,CR,EC k

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Perspective makes far vehicles tiny. In bird's-eye view that variation mostly disappears, so less data is needed.
  • Arm and ETA become learned outputs, matching the lane-less, arm-level objective.

Risk

  • Tall vehicles stretch in bird's-eye view. We use the ground-contact point, plus camera-view crops for appearance.
09

ChirpDoppler-Net — Physics-Informed Siren Network

Filters shaped like siren sweeps detect the siren, and a Doppler branch tells whether it is coming closer and roughly when it will arrive.

Physics-basedMore lead timeFewer false alarms
flowchart LR
  A["Audio, 16 kHz"] --> CH["Learnable chirplet filters
500–2,500 Hz sweeps"]
  CH --> T["Small TCN"] --> S["Siren / no siren"]
  CH --> D["Doppler branch
pitch shift + loudness rise"]
  D --> AP["Approaching / receding"]
  D --> ET["Audio ETA estimate"]
  classDef k fill:#ffffff,stroke:#1b1f24,color:#1b1f24
  classDef g fill:#fbeceb,stroke:#b4232c,color:#1b1f24
  class A,CH,T,S,D k
  class AP,ET g

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Physics fixes most of the model, so very few parameters are learnt.
  • Ignoring sirens that are moving away cuts false alarms per hour.
  • An early audio ETA increases lead time, the project's headline metric.

Risk

  • The Doppler shift is small at low speed and is affected by echoes, so it is used as a soft feature. One microphone cannot give direction, so the arm still comes from vision.

JunctionMAE — Self-Supervised Junction Encoder

Learns what a normal junction looks and sounds like from hours of unlabeled recordings, then needs only a few labels for the final task.

Why it fits SirenFirst

  • Unlabeled junction footage is easy and legal to record; labels are needed only for the small heads.
  • Still counts as training from scratch: our own weights, trained on our own data.

Risk

  • Pretraining takes the most GPU time, roughly 10–20 hours split across Kaggle sessions.
